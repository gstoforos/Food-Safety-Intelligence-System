AFTS-FSIS contains notices spanning 86 jurisdictions and 43 source labels. Thirty-five labels represent official or regulatory publishers assigned a coverage class; eight aggregator or non-regulatory labels are excluded. Six continuously collected publishers determine the analytical coverage window. Every notice is normalised into one schema and screened for pathogens, biotoxins, mycotoxins, foreign material, pest and chemical hazards.

The complete 35-week corpus contains 1,543 notices. The approved 12-week analytical window contains 724 — about 60 a week. Those are two different denominators, and the rest of this page keeps them apart.

Nobody reads approximately 60 notices a week across the monitored feeds and spots that one pathogen in one country has quietly doubled against its own recent baseline. That is the job the detector does.

The clearest example is Listeria monocytogenes in France, which cleared the threshold in 3 separate weeks (13 Jul 2026, 20 Jul 2026, 10 Aug 2026) — 37 notices against a baseline of 12.0 in the first of them. A sustained elevation in one country, identified in the walk-forward replay for the week in which the elevation began, inside a stream averaging 60 notices a week that nobody reads end to end.

Why counting is not enough

Here is the trap. The single largest weekly jump in this corpus is the week of 6 Apr 2026: +63 notices, of which 43 came from RappelConso (FR) alone. Any tool that watches raw counts alarms on that, and on every hazard class inside it, simultaneously. The pattern is strongly consistent with a publisher-volume event rather than evidence of a comparable change across the food supply.

AFTS-FSIS scores each stratum on its share of the week's total output rather than its raw count. When a publisher doubles everything it files, every share stays roughly where it was and the detector stays quiet. When one pathogen in one country rises against a flat total, the share moves and the detector does not stay quiet.

The question is never “are there more notices this week?” It is “is this hazard taking a larger share of the monitored regulatory corpus than it did a month ago?”

Detect, then attribute

A share signal is not automatically a food-safety event either, and this is where most tools stop and AFTS-FSIS does not. 7 of the 10 elevations above came predominantly from a single publisher — and every one of those carries that fact in the record, with the publisher named and its percentage stated. The 11 further rows that moved on count but not on share are kept in a separate ledger and labelled as what they are: publisher-review items, not findings.

You get the signal and the reason to doubt it in the same row. An analyst can act on one and dismiss the other in seconds, which is the difference between a feed that gets read every morning and one that gets muted in week three.

What we do not claim

  • This is not prediction. The corpus has no denominator — no production volumes, no negative examples. Nothing here forecasts which product will be recalled next.
  • Notice counts are not incidence. A diligent regulator looks worse than a quiet one. Every stratum is compared only against itself.
  • No outcome linkage. We do not claim any of these signals predicted a confirmed outbreak. They are elevations in the regulatory record, detected and attributed.
  • Some events are invisible to us. An outbreak identified through clinical surveillance may generate no notice until the investigation closes. Nothing in the corpus can anticipate that.

1What this is, and what it is not

This is a retrospective aberration detector over regulatory recall notices. It answers one question: is a given stratum — a pathogen, a country, a hazard tier, or a combination — running above its own recent baseline this week?

It does not forecast. The corpus has no denominator: no production volumes, no consumption data, no negative examples. Nothing here supports a statement of the form “product X is likely to be recalled”, and no output string in the module makes one. A rise in notices is first a fact about publishers and only second a fact about food.

The corpus covers 35 complete weeks, 29 Dec 2025 to 24 Aug 2026, holding 1,543 notices. The trailing partial week is dropped at load: a week still being populated always looks like a decline, and including it would both suppress genuine signals and poison every baseline it later enters.

2Method

2.1 Detector

CDC EARS C1/C2/C3 (Hutwagner et al., 2003), chosen over Farrington (1996) and Noufaily (2013) because those generally require multiple years of stable historical data to model seasonality reliably, and this corpus holds 35 weeks. The stratum contract is designed to survive a migration to Farrington without changing callers, once the history exists.

  • C1 — baseline is the 7 weeks immediately preceding the test week.
  • C2 — same width, offset by a 2-week guard band, so a slow-onset event cannot quietly raise the baseline it is being measured against. Both channels read this same window: for the week commencing 8 Jun 2026 the baseline is 6 Apr 2026–18 May 2026, with 25 May 2026 and 1 Jun 2026 held out.
  • C3 — thresholded excess accumulated over the last 3 weeks, which catches sustained low-grade drift that no single week would trip. The implementation is exactly C3t = Σi=0..2 max(0, C2t-i − 1), each C2 recomputed against its own baseline window — not a plain sum of C2 values.

The corpus's first 12 weeks therefore produce no output, which is why the replay below starts where it does. That figure is an implementation requirement, not an inherent property of EARS. The code guards with 7 + 2 + 3, a readable expression that is one week conservative: the binding constraint is the earliest week the oldest C2 in the C3 sum can reach, which is t − 2 − (7 + 2), making 11 leading weeks the arithmetic minimum. One further week of history is discarded than strictly necessary. Nothing is wrong with the scored weeks; there is simply one fewer of them than there could be.

2.2 Two channels, and why the share channel is the one that alarms

RappelConso (FR) and RASFF (EU) together supply 76% of all records. When one of them clears a backlog, every raw count in the corpus rises at once, and a count-only detector alarms on all of them simultaneously — a publisher event wearing the costume of a food-safety event.

So two channels are computed. The count channel compares the raw weekly count against its baseline mean and standard deviation; it is diagnostic. The share channel compares the stratum's proportion of that week's whole corpus against the pooled baseline share, by exact binomial test; this is the channel that alarms.

It is tempting to say shares are invariant to publisher volume, and an earlier draft of this report did. That is too strong. The share channel partially normalises changes in total weekly volume, although it remains sensitive to changes in publisher mix and to publisher-specific hazard composition - a publisher whose output skews towards one hazard class moves that class's share when it moves its volume. A count signal with no matching share signal is therefore reported as a potentially volume-driven publication event requiring publisher-level review, not as a finding.

Effect follows the channel

Until 27 August 2026 the effect column carried the share ratio for every signal, including count-only ones. Share rows were unaffected; the count-only rows were not, so inside the analytical window the reported effect changes on 11 of 21 rows. One of those changes was disqualifying rather than cosmetic: a count-only Listeria signal for France carried a share ratio of 0.85 — below one — telling the reader the stratum fell in the same row that said it alarmed. The effect column now follows the channel. Both ratios are retained on every record, so no previously published figure becomes unrecoverable.

2.3 Sparsity ladder

Most pathogen × country cells hold under one record a week. Testing them directly turns 1→3 into a “threefold jump”. Each stratum is therefore tested only at the finest level whose baseline mean clears MIN_BASELINE_MEAN and whose observed count clears MIN_ABSOLUTE_COUNT; otherwise it rolls up, country → region → global.

2.4 Multiplicity

440 strata are evaluated per run. At α = 0.01 that could produce approximately 4.4 nominal positives per run under an all-null approximation with independent tests — the strata are not independent, since a child and its parent share records, so treat that as an order of magnitude rather than an expectation. Either way it is enough to train a reader to ignore the feed, which is the failure mode that matters most for an operational product. Benjamini–Hochberg FDR at q = 0.10 is applied across all strata within a run, and the output is capped at 12 signals ranked by effect.

2.5 Ranking, and what the output cap actually does

§5.1 warns against reading the two channels as one scale, which raises a fair question about the cap: if output is limited to 12 rows “ranked by effect”, are share ratios and count ratios being ranked against each other? They are not. The sort key is (channel != "proportion", -effect): the first element separates the channels into blocks, and the effect ordering applies only within a block. A share ratio is never compared to a count ratio.

Two further facts, both measurable rather than asserted. The cap has never bound on this corpus: across all 23 scored weeks, the largest post-deduplication output was 6 rows against a cap of 12. And the gap a reader may notice between “survived FDR” and “reported” in the run metadata is not the cap — it is the parent/child collapse, which drops a parent stratum when a child accounts for 80% or more of it. The run metadata now carries after_dedup and cap_binding so the two cannot be confused.

3.1 Maturity dates are frozen once determined

Why maturity is frozen

Maturity was originally computed against the most recent eight weeks — a moving reference. The consequence was not drift but oscillation: one source's maturity moved five times across roughly eleven weeks, and another alternated between two dates eight weeks apart. Because the analytical window opens 9 weeks after the latest maturity among continuous sources, a single source flipping could have moved the window forward by eight weeks and silently excluded six of the seven signal weeks in this report.

Maturity is now frozen at first determination and carries its basis. A determination made on an unripe window is marked provisional and is not frozen. --verify-stability exits non-zero if a rebuild would move any cutoff, so this cannot regress unobserved.

3.2 The analytical window

The latest maturity among continuously collected sources is 6 Apr 2026. A test week is only defensible once its entire baseline lies after that date, which is 7 baseline weeks plus 2 guard weeks later. The window therefore opens 8 Jun 2026.

Two things about that date are worth stating plainly. It is derived by code, not asserted: signal_detector.coverage_window_start() returns it from the register. And until 27 August 2026 the detector contained no reference to the coverage register at all — the window in the first draft of this report had been applied by hand. The gate is now a property of the code. It is deliberately additive: signals outside the window are flagged, not dropped, unless a caller asks for enforcement, because silently changing what a detector reports is a worse cure than the disease.

5Results: walk-forward replay

Every week is scored using only weeks strictly before it. This is not cross-validation — random folds on surveillance time series leak the future into the baseline and produce beautiful, meaningless numbers. 23 weeks were scored; the first 12 are consumed by the baseline, the guard band and the C3 accumulation window (7 + 2 + 3; see §2.1) and cannot be scored at all.

There is no labelled anomaly set, so these tables are not an accuracy measurement. They are the alarm ledger: each row is reviewed by hand and marked genuine or artefact, and that log becomes the calibration record.

5.1 How a row is admitted — the two channels differ

This matters for reading the last column, and it is not symmetric. A share row is admitted by an exact binomial test at α = 0.01 and must then survive Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.10 across every stratum tested that week. A count-only row is admitted by the EARS statistic alone — C1 or C2 at 3.0, or C3 at 2.0, with the observed count at least 20% above baseline — and bypasses FDR by design, because it is not being offered as a discovery. It is logged as a potentially volume-driven publication event requiring publisher-level review, and suppressing those by multiplicity control would hide exactly the rows an operator needs in order to recognise a backlog dump.

Because they are not multiplicity-controlled, count-channel rows carry no false-discovery guarantee of any kind, and no count of them should be reported as a number of signals. Every summary figure in this report separates the two.

Do not read the two channels as one scale

A count-only row carries a binomial p-value in the underlying record, and that number is frequently near 1.0 — which is the point: the count rose while the share did not, so the share test correctly declines to alarm. Reporting that p-value beside a count-only row would invite the reader to conclude the row is meaningless, when what it actually documents is a publication event. The column below therefore shows each row's own admission criterion, never the other channel's.

6Limitations

No denominator. Notice counts are not incidence. A country that publishes diligently will look worse than one that does not, and this detector cannot distinguish the two. Every stratum is compared only against itself.

Short history. 35 weeks supports EARS and nothing seasonal. Any pattern with an annual period is invisible, and a genuine seasonal rise will be read as an aberration until multiple years of stable history exist.

Publisher events dominate the count channel. Mitigated by the share channel, not eliminated. A backlog clearance concentrated in one hazard class will still move a share.

Outbreaks that never generate a notice are invisible. Some outbreaks surface through clinical surveillance rather than product testing, and the implicated commodity is identified only after case interviews converge. A recent multi-state Cyclospora cluster is the clean example: AFTS-FSIS observes the regulatory record, and where no notice is issued until an investigation concludes, there is nothing in the corpus for the detector to see. This is a structural limit of recall-derived signal, not a tuning failure, and no amount of threshold work addresses it.

Coverage inference is not ground truth. Onset and maturity are inferred from publication behaviour because no regulator publishes a machine-readable statement of when its own feed became complete. §4 shows how much the inference depends on constants that were chosen by judgement.

7.1 What reproducibility means here, precisely

The page is not byte-for-byte reproducible, and it would be dishonest to claim otherwise: it stamps its own build time, so two builds always differ. Corpus revision alone would not be sufficient even without that, because the result also depends on the detector's source revision, its constants, the coverage register, and the runtime.

What is pinned is the analysis. The digest below is SHA-256 over the analytical payload with the clock excluded — corpus shape, coverage window, every constant in §2.6, and every replay row with its observed count, baseline, both effect ratios and p-value:

8Revision history

The method is versioned, and corrections to it are listed rather than absorbed. Each entry names what changed and whether reported results moved, so a figure quoted from an earlier build can be placed.

27 Aug 2026 Guard band aligned across channels.
