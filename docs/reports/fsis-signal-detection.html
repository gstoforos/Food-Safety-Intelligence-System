How FSIS tells a food-safety signal from a publisher dump

Recall volume goes up for two very different reasons. One of them matters. Over 22 weeks of monitored output we found 12 statistically controlled elevations — and named the publisher behind every one of them.

FSIS reads recall and alert notices from 35 regulatory publishers, normalises them into one schema, and screens them for pathogens, biotoxins, mycotoxins, foreign material, pest and chemical hazards. Across the analysable window that is 1,506 notices and about 62 a week.

Nobody reads 62 notices a week across 35 publishers and spots that one pathogen in one country has quietly doubled against its own recent baseline. That is the job the detector does.

The clearest example is Listeria monocytogenes in France, which cleared the threshold in 3 separate weeks (13 Jul 2026, 20 Jul 2026, 10 Aug 2026) — 37 notices against a baseline of 12.0 in the first of them. A sustained elevation in one country, flagged in the week it began, inside a stream averaging 62 notices a week that nobody reads end to end.

Why counting is not enough

Here is the trap. The single largest weekly jump in this corpus is the week of 6 Apr 2026: +63 notices, of which 43 came from RappelConso (FR) alone. Any tool that watches raw counts alarms on that, and on every hazard class inside it, simultaneously. Nothing happened to the food supply that week. A publisher cleared a backlog.

FSIS scores each stratum on its share of the week's total output rather than its raw count. When a publisher doubles everything it files, every share stays roughly where it was and the detector stays quiet. When one pathogen in one country rises against a flat total, the share moves and the detector does not stay quiet.

The question is never “are there more notices this week?” It is “is this hazard taking a larger share of the world's regulatory attention than it did a month ago?”

Detect, then attribute

A share signal is not automatically a food-safety event either, and this is where most tools stop and FSIS does not. 7 of the 12 elevations above came predominantly from a single publisher — and every one of those carries that fact in the record, with the publisher named and its percentage stated. The 8 further rows that moved on count but not on share are kept in a separate ledger and labelled as what they are: publisher-review items, not findings.

You get the signal and the reason to doubt it in the same row. An analyst can act on one and dismiss the other in seconds, which is the difference between a feed that gets read every morning and one that gets muted in week three.

What we do not claim

  • This is not prediction. The corpus has no denominator — no production volumes, no negative examples. Nothing here forecasts which product will be recalled next.
  • Notice counts are not incidence. A diligent regulator looks worse than a quiet one. Every stratum is compared only against itself.
  • No outcome linkage. We do not claim any of these signals predicted a confirmed outbreak. They are elevations in the regulatory record, detected and attributed.
  • Some events are invisible to us. An outbreak identified through clinical surveillance may generate no notice until the investigation closes. Nothing in the corpus can anticipate that.
